Arterial Blood Gas Calculator: Acid-Base Balance Interpretation

History of blood gas analysis, physiological parameters, interpretation of acid-base disorders, and interactive calculator for complete analysis.

1. Introduction

Arterial blood gas analysis is a fundamental laboratory test in clinical practice and nursing, used to assess the patient's acid-base balance, oxygenation, and pulmonary ventilation. By analyzing parameters such as pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃⁻, and PaO₂, blood gas analysis allows the identification of disorders such as acidosis and alkalosis — respiratory or metabolic — and guides immediate therapeutic interventions in critical settings such as ICUs and emergency departments (SMELTZER; BARE, 2020).

2. History of Arterial Blood Gas Analysis

19th Century — Max von Pettenkofer
The German chemist Max von Pettenkofer (1818–1901) was one of the pioneers in measuring carbon dioxide (CO₂) in biological samples. His work established the foundations for understanding respiratory gas metabolism and the relationship between ventilation and acid-base balance (SEVERINGHAUS; ASTRUP, 1987).
1952 — Copenhagen Polio Epidemic
During the severe polio epidemic that struck Copenhagen, Denmark, anesthesiologist Bjarne Ibsen introduced manual positive-pressure ventilation for patients with respiratory paralysis. To monitor ventilation effectiveness, Poul Astrup, a biochemist at Copenhagen Hospital, developed methods to measure blood pH and CO₂, laying the foundations of modern blood gas analysis (SEVERINGHAUS; ASTRUP, 1987; WEST, 2015).
1954 — Radiometer Founded
The Danish company Radiometer was founded in Copenhagen by Poul Astrup and colleagues, commercializing the world's first blood gas analyzer — the Astrup Apparatus. This equipment revolutionized intensive care by enabling rapid bedside measurement of pH, pCO₂, and bicarbonate (SEVERINGHAUS; ASTRUP, 1987).
1960s–1970s — Clark and Severinghaus Electrodes
Leland C. Clark developed the polarographic oxygen electrode (Clark electrode, 1956), and John W. Severinghaus perfected the CO₂ electrode. The combination of these electrodes with the glass pH electrode resulted in modern blood gas analyzers that simultaneously measure pH, pO₂, and pCO₂ (WEST, 2015).
Present Day
Modern blood gas analyzers are automated devices that, from an arterial blood sample (0.5–1.0 mL), provide dozens of parameters within minutes: pH, PaCO₂, PaO₂, HCO₃⁻, BE (base excess), SatO₂, electrolytes (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺), lactate, glucose, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and co-oximetry (COHb, MetHb). These devices are present in virtually all hospitals with intensive care units (SMELTZER; BARE, 2020).

Region of Origin

Modern blood gas analysis was born in Copenhagen, Denmark, as a result of the collaboration between anesthesiologist Bjarne Ibsen and biochemist Poul Astrup during the 1952 polio epidemic. The company Radiometer, founded in the same city in 1954, remains to this day one of the world's leading manufacturers of blood gas analyzers.

3. Arterial Blood Gas Parameters

Arterial blood gas analysis evaluates three fundamental axes: acid-base balance (pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃⁻), oxygenation (PaO₂, SatO₂), and tissue perfusion (lactate). The integrated interpretation of these parameters allows nurses and the multidisciplinary team to quickly identify severe clinical alterations (SMELTZER; BARE, 2020; GOLDWASSER et al., 2018).

pH7.35–7.45Acidemia (<7.35) or alkalemia (>7.45). Reflects the overall acid-base status.

HCO₃⁻22–26 mEq/LMetabolic component. ↓ = metabolic acidosis; ↑ = metabolic alkalosis.
PaO₂80–100 mmHgArterial oxygenation. <60 mmHg = severe hypoxemia (respiratory failure).

SatO₂95–100%Arterial oxygen saturation. Correlates with PaO₂ via the hemoglobin dissociation curve.
Lactate<2.0 mmol/LMarker of tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism. ↑ = risk of shock.

4. Interpretation of Acid-Base Disorders

Blood gas interpretation follows a systematic 4-step method (GOLDWASSER et al., 2018):

Step 1 — Assess the pH

pH < 7.35: Acidemia (acidosis). The body has an excess of acids or a loss of bases.
pH > 7.45: Alkalemia (alkalosis). The body has an excess of bases or a loss of acids.
pH 7.35–7.45: Normal or fully compensated.

Step 2 — Identify the Primary Disorder

Correlate the pH with PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ to determine if the disorder is respiratory (primary alteration of PaCO₂) or metabolic (primary alteration of HCO₃⁻):

DisorderpHPaCO₂HCO₃⁻Common Causes
Respiratory Acidosis↓ (<7.35)↑ (>45)Normal or ↑COPD, respiratory depression, muscle fatigue, severe asthma
Respiratory Alkalosis↑ (>7.45)↓ (<35)Normal or ↓Hyperventilation, anxiety, pain, pulmonary embolism, altitude
Metabolic Acidosis↓ (<7.35)Normal or ↓↓ (<22)Diabetic ketoacidosis, renal failure, severe diarrhea, shock, methanol poisoning
Metabolic Alkalosis↑ (>7.45)Normal or ↑↑ (>26)Vomiting, gastric suction, excessive diuretic use, hypokalemia

Step 3 — Assess Compensation

The body attempts to compensate for the primary disorder through the opposite system (respiratory compensates for metabolic and vice versa). Compensation can be (GOLDWASSER et al., 2018):

  • Uncompensated: abnormal pH and only the primary system altered.
  • Partially compensated: pH still abnormal, but the opposite system has already initiated a response.
  • Fully compensated: normalized pH, but both systems altered (e.g., chronic COPD with elevated HCO₃⁻ maintaining normal pH).

Step 4 — Calculate the Anion Gap

The Anion Gap (AG) is calculated using the formula: AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻). An elevated AG (>12 mEq/L) indicates the presence of unmeasurable anions (lactate, ketoacids, toxins) and is characteristic of metabolic acidoses with an increased anion gap (GOLDWASSER et al., 2018).

📊 Remember: The classic causes of metabolic acidosis with an elevated anion gap can be remembered by the mnemonic MUDPILES: Methanol, Uremia, Diabetes (ketoacidosis), Paraldehyde, Isoniazid, Lactate, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates.

5. Importance for Nursing

Arterial blood gas analysis is a routine test in critical care units, and its interpretation is the responsibility of the nursing team, which should (SMELTZER; BARE, 2020):

  1. Properly collect the sample: Aseptic technique, correct heparinization of the syringe, elimination of air bubbles, and immediate transport on ice (when not processed within 15 minutes).
  2. Perform the modified Allen test: Before puncturing the radial artery, check collateral circulation via the ulnar artery to avoid distal ischemia.
  3. Monitor complications: Hematoma, thrombosis, arteriovenous fistula, and infection at the puncture site.
  4. Interpret the results: Early identification of acid-base and oxygenation changes indicating clinical deterioration.
  5. Communicate with the team: Immediately report critical changes to the medical team such as severe acidosis (pH < 7.20), hypercapnia (PaCO₂ > 60 mmHg), or severe hypoxemia (PaO₂ < 60 mmHg).
🧪 Relevant fact: Arterial blood gas analysis is considered the gold standard for assessing oxygenation and ventilation, being superior to pulse oximetry in situations of poor peripheral perfusion, severe anemia, carbon monoxide poisoning (COHb), and methemoglobinemia (GOLDWASSER et al., 2018).

6. Summary

The arterial blood gas analysis, whose foundations were established by Poul Astrup and colleagues in Copenhagen in the 1950s during the polio epidemic, is now an essential test in clinical practice. Through the integrated analysis of pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃⁻, PaO₂, lactate, and anion gap, it is possible to identify acid-base disorders (respiratory/metabolic acidosis/alkalosis), assess tissue oxygenation, and guide immediate interventions in critically ill patients. The systematic interpretation in 4 steps — pH, primary disorder, compensation, and anion gap — constitutes a fundamental competence for nurses working in intensive care and emergency units (SEVERINGHAUS; ASTRUP, 1987; SMELTZER; BARE, 2020; GOLDWASSER et al., 2018).
