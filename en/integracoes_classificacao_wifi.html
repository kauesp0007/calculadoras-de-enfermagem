1. Introduction

The WIfI Classification (Wound, Ischemia, foot Infection) is a risk stratification system developed by the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) of the United States for the assessment of threatened lower limbs in patients with peripheral arterial disease and diabetic foot. Originally published in 2014 in the Journal of Vascular Surgery (MILLS et al., 2014), the classification was developed by the SVS Lower Extremity Guidelines Committee with the aim of standardizing language across medical specialties — vascular surgeons, endocrinologists, infectious disease specialists, orthopedic surgeons, and nurses — and enabling objective comparisons of clinical outcomes across different centers and studies (MILLS et al., 2014, p. 221).

Unlike previous classifications that focused on only one aspect of the disease — such as the Fontaine classification (ischemia), the Wagner classification (wound), or the IDSA classification (infection) — the WIfI system integrates three independent axes that, when combined, determine the overall risk of major amputation within one year and the likelihood of benefit from revascularization (MILLS et al., 2014). Mills et al. (2014, p. 222) state that "the WIfI classification was designed to stratify the risk of amputation based on three key factors: wound, ischemia, and foot infection, each graded on a scale from 0 to 3."

3. The Three Components of the WIfI Classification

The classification assesses three independent parameters, each graded from 0 to 3, where 0 = normal/absent and 3 = severe impairment. The combination of W, I, and fI grades determines the WIfI clinical stage (1 to 4), which in turn estimates the 1-year amputation risk and the expected benefit of revascularization (MILLS et al., 2014, p. 223).

ComponentGradeDescription
W — Wound0No ulcer, no gangrene (ischemic rest pain may be present)
1Small, shallow ulcer on the distal leg or foot; no bone exposure unless limited to the distal phalanx; no gangrene or gangrene limited to the distal phalanx
2Deep ulcer with exposed bone, joint, or tendon; generally does not involve the heel; gangrene limited to the toes
3Extensive, deep ulcer involving the forefoot and/or midfoot; deep heel ulceration (± calcaneus); extensive gangrene involving the forefoot and/or midfoot; extensive heel necrosis
I — Ischemia0ABI ≥ 0.80; ankle systolic pressure > 100 mmHg; toe pressure (TP) or TcPO₂ ≥ 60 mmHg
1ABI 0.60-0.79; ankle systolic pressure 70-100 mmHg; TP/TcPO₂ 40-59 mmHg
2ABI 0.40-0.59; ankle systolic pressure 50-70 mmHg; TP/TcPO₂ 30-39 mmHg
3ABI ≤ 0.39; ankle systolic pressure < 50 mmHg; TP/TcPO₂ < 30 mmHg
fI — foot Infection0No signs or symptoms of infection; no evidence of active infection
1Local infection involving only skin and subcutaneous tissue (no deep tissue involvement, no systemic signs); erythema > 0.5 cm and ≤ 2 cm around the ulcer
2Local infection with erythema > 2 cm, or involving deep structures (abscess, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, fasciitis); without SIRS (Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome)
3Local infection with signs of SIRS (≥ 2 criteria: temperature > 38°C or < 36°C; HR > 90 bpm; RR > 20 breaths/min or PaCO₂ < 32 mmHg; leukocytes > 12,000 or < 4,000/µL or > 10% band forms)

Source: Adapted from MILLS et al. (2014, p. 223-225). ABI = Ankle-Brachial Index; TP = Toe Pressure; TcPO₂ = Transcutaneous Oxygen Pressure; SIRS = Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome.

Attention: The WIfI classification should be applied by a trained professional. The interpretation of scores and the definition of clinical management — especially the indication for revascularization and amputation — are within the competence of the vascular physician (MILLS et al., 2014; SBACV, 2020).

7. Treatment and Clinical Conduct

The management of the threatened limb requires a multidisciplinary approach involving vascular surgery, endocrinology, infectious diseases, orthopedics, and specialized wound nursing. Mills et al. (2014, p. 230) emphasize that "the WIfI classification was not designed to dictate treatment, but to provide a framework that allows meaningful comparisons of outcomes between different therapeutic modalities".

Lower Limb Angioplasty Exams

The assessment of arterial perfusion is mandatory in the WIfI classification. Complementary exams include (MILLS et al., 2014; CONTE et al., 2019):

  • ABI (Ankle-Brachial Index): First-line, non-invasive test. Values < 0.90 indicate peripheral artery disease; < 0.40 indicate critical ischemia.
  • Hallux Pressure (TP): More reliable in diabetics (calcified tibial arteries may falsely elevate ABI). TP < 30 mmHg indicates critical ischemia.
  • TcPO₂ (Transcutaneous O₂ Pressure): Predicts wound healing. TcPO₂ < 30 mmHg indicates low probability of healing without revascularization.
  • Arterial Doppler ultrasound: Maps vascular anatomy and identifies stenoses/occlusions.
  • Angiography (catheter or CT/MR angiography): Gold standard for surgical planning of revascularization.

Nursing Conduct in Diabetic Foot

The nurse plays a central role in the serial assessment and care of patients with diabetic foot classified by WIfI (SBACV, 2020; INTERNATIONAL WORKING GROUP ON THE DIABETIC FOOT [IWGDF], 2019):

  • Systematic foot assessment: Daily inspection, palpation of pedal and posterior tibial pulses, sensitivity testing with 10g monofilament (Semmes-Weinstein) and 128 Hz tuning fork.
  • Classification and documentation: Apply the WIfI classification and photographically record the wound's evolution.
  • Specialized dressings: Select coverings according to the degree of exudate, depth, and presence of infection — hydrocolloids, alginates, hydrofibers, foams, activated charcoal with silver.
  • Negative pressure therapy (NPT): Indicated for extensive wounds with abundant exudate (W2-W3).
  • Pressure offloading: Use of therapeutic footwear, total contact casting (TCC), or orthotics to redistribute plantar pressure.
  • Patient and family education: Guidance on foot self-care, glycemic control, smoking cessation, and early identification of warning signs.
  • Post-revascularization monitoring: Assess distal perfusion, limb temperature, color, and capillary refill.

8. Prognosis

The prognosis of the patient with diabetic foot classified by WIfI depends on three main factors: metabolic control (HbA1c < 7%), successful revascularization when indicated, and infection control (IWGDF, 2019). Validation studies have shown that (ZETTERVALL et al., 2015; DARLING et al., 2016):

  • Patients with WIfI Stage 1-2 have a limb salvage rate greater than 95% in 1 year with adequate clinical management.
  • Patients with WIfI Stage 3 have a higher amputation rate of approximately 10-15% in 1 year, even with revascularization.
  • Patients with WIfI Stage 4 have a higher amputation rate exceeding 30% and 1-year mortality of 20-25%, often due to cardiovascular events.
  • The presence of severe infection (fI3) is the strongest isolated predictor of immediate major amputation.
Favorable prognosis: WIfI 1-2 with adequate glycemic control (HbA1c < 7%), timely revascularization, and absence of severe infection. Limb salvage rate: > 90%.

9. Prevention: Diabetes Control, Nutrition, Sedentary Lifestyle, and Smoking Cessation

The prevention of diabetic foot and progression to advanced stages of WIfI is based on four modifiable pillars (IWGDF, 2019; BRAZILIAN DIABETES SOCIETY [SBD], 2023):

9.1 Strict Glycemic Control

  • HbA1c target: < 7.0% for adults; individualize for elderly and patients with multiple comorbidities (SBD, 2023).
  • Monitoring: Pre-prandial capillary blood glucose (80-130 mg/dL) and post-prandial (< 180 mg/dL).
  • Medication adherence: Oral hypoglycemics (metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists) and insulin therapy as prescribed.
  • The UKPDS study (1998) demonstrated that a 1% reduction in HbA1c reduces 37% of microvascular complications, including neuropathy and foot ulcers.

9.2 Adequate Nutrition

  • Balanced diet with 45-60% low glycemic index carbohydrates, 15-20% proteins, and 20-35% unsaturated fats (SBD, 2023).
  • Restriction of simple carbohydrates (sugar, white flour, soft drinks) and saturated/trans fats.
  • Consumption of fiber (≥ 25 g/day), fruits, vegetables, and whole grains.
  • Fractionation of meals into 5-6 daily portions to avoid glycemic spikes.

9.3 Combating Sedentary Lifestyle

  • Minimum recommendation of 150 minutes/week of moderate aerobic activity (walking, swimming, cycling) or 75 minutes/week of vigorous activity (WHO, 2020).
  • Muscle strengthening exercises 2-3 times a week.
  • Regular physical activity improves insulin sensitivity, reduces HbA1c by 0.6-0.8%, and improves peripheral circulation — a protective factor against ischemia (IWGDF, 2019).
  • Important: Patients with neuropathy should avoid high-impact exercises and walking barefoot to prevent foot trauma.

9.4 Smoking Cessation

  • Smoking is the main modifiable risk factor for peripheral artery disease and progression of ischemia in WIfI (MILLS et al., 2014).
  • Smokers have a 2-4 times higher risk of developing critical ischemia and major amputation compared to non-smokers.
  • Smoking cessation reduces the risk of amputation by 50% in 5 years (IWGDF, 2019).
  • Strategies: nicotine replacement therapy (patches, gums), bupropion, varenicline, and psychological support.

10. Summary

The WIfI Classification, developed by the Lower Extremity Guidelines Committee of the Society for Vascular Surgery and published by Mills et al. (2014) in the Journal of Vascular Surgery, is the international standard for risk stratification of amputation in threatened lower limbs. Composed of three parameters — Wound, Ischemia, and foot Infection — each graded from 0 to 3, the classification allows estimating the risk of major amputation in 1 year (Table IV) and the expected benefit of revascularization (Table V), provided that the infection is controlled. Clinical conduct should be defined by the vascular physician based on the WIfI stage (1 to 4) and requires a multidisciplinary approach involving nursing, endocrinology, infectious diseases, and orthopedics. Prevention is based on strict glycemic control, balanced nutrition, regular physical activity, and smoking cessation. Its practical application is available in the online calculator on this site (MILLS et al., 2014; CONTE et al., 2019; IWGDF, 2019; SBACV, 2020).
