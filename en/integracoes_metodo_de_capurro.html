Capurro Method: Clinical Estimation of Gestational Age

Evidence-based educational content for safe application in neonatal care.

1. What is the Capurro Method and why does it matter?

The Capurro Method is a clinical assessment tool used to estimate the gestational age (GA) of a newborn based on signs of somatic and neurological maturity. In nursing practice, it is particularly useful when the date of the last menstrual period (LMP) is uncertain and when early ultrasound is either unavailable or was not performed in a timely manner.

The accurate estimation of GA guides critical decisions in the first hours of life, such as anticipating the risk of hypothermia, hypoglycemia, respiratory distress, identifying the need for intensive monitoring, and prioritizing supportive care interventions.

2. Scientific foundation (Literature Summary)

The scientific literature reviewed reinforces three central messages for neonatal care:

  1. Capurro is highly viable in real-world settings: An academic-clinical experience report (UFFS, 2017) describes the scale as a non-invasive, easily executed method that is highly useful for individualizing the newborn's care plan right in the labor and delivery unit.
  2. Variations between methods are expected: A Brazilian study assessing postpartum women and newborns (REAS, 2021) found an average difference of up to 7 days between LMP, Capurro, and Ultrasound, which does not invalidate the clinical utility of Capurro as a practical estimate.
  3. Interpretation requires critical thinking: A study involving 309 mother-infant dyads (Rev. Bras. Saúde Materno Infantil, 2011) showed a tendency for the method to slightly overestimate or underestimate depending on the gestational age bracket, highlighting that no single method should be used in a vacuum without clinical context.

3. When to use the Capurro Method in clinical workflow

  • Newborns without reliable obstetric dates for GA calculation.
  • Absence of a first-trimester ultrasound.
  • Need for rapid risk stratification within the first hours of life.
  • Transitions of care in resource-limited maternity settings.

When there is a significant discrepancy between the Capurro assessment and robust obstetric data (e.g., early ultrasound combined with a consistent clinical history), the multidisciplinary team must prioritize integrated clinical judgment and serial clinical monitoring.

Detailed explanation of the 6 items of the Somatic-Neurological Capurro

  1. Skin texture (somatic): Evaluates thickness, opacity, and peeling. Very thin and translucent skin suggests lesser maturity; thicker skin with mild peeling tends to indicate a higher GA. Nurses should perform this assessment under good lighting and avoid confusing transient hydration changes with actual maturity.
  2. Ear form / Pinna firmness (somatic): Assesses the consistency of the ear cartilage and the speed of recoil after gently folding the pinna. A soft pinna with slow recoil points to immaturity; a firm pinna with instant recoil indicates greater maturity. The maneuver must be gentle to prevent discomfort and bias from localized edema.
  3. Breast size / Breast bud (somatic): Considers the palpable diameter of the breast tissue. A more prominent and easily palpable breast bud correlates with higher maturity. Avoid excessive palpation; the goal is clinical measurement, not repetitive manipulation.
  4. Plantar creases (somatic): Analyzes the extent and depth of the creases on the soles of the feet. In preterm infants, creases are sparse or absent; in more mature newborns, they cover a larger plantar area. Both feet should be observed and findings recorded objectively to reduce inter-rater variability.
  5. Scarf sign (neurological): By gently drawing the newborn's upper extremity across the chest, the nurse assesses muscle resistance and the endpoint of the elbow. Less resistance (elbow crosses the midline easily) suggests immaturity; greater resistance implies higher neurological maturity. It is essential to respect joint limits and never force range of motion.
  6. Head lag (neurological): During a controlled pull-to-sit maneuver, the degree of head control is observed. A pronounced head drop (lagging behind the trunk) indicates lower muscle tone and greater immaturity; better head support suggests higher maturity. The newborn's safety is paramount: this maneuver must be brief and performed with adequate head support.

5. How to apply at the bedside (step-by-step)

  1. Ensure baseline clinical stability of the newborn and prepare a neutral thermal environment.
  2. Perform the somatic and neurological assessment using a standardized technique, documenting findings immediately.
  3. Sum the score of the six criteria according to the adopted institutional protocol or formula.
  4. Convert the score into weeks of GA and classify the newborn (preterm, term, or post-term).
  5. Compare the result with available obstetric data and document any relevant discrepancies.
  6. Adjust the nursing care plan based on the estimated maturity and clinical risk.

6. Clinical interpretation and limitations

Literature indicates that the Capurro method is valuable for initial decision-making, but accuracy can vary depending on the gestational age range and the newborn's clinical condition. Notably:

  • It may overestimate GA in certain brackets and underestimate in others;
  • Edema, perinatal complications, and delayed assessment can alter the reading of physical signs;
  • Early ultrasound remains the gold standard reference when available.

In practice, the Capurro method should be viewed as a complementary clinical tool, rather than an absolute substitute for a comprehensive obstetric evaluation.
