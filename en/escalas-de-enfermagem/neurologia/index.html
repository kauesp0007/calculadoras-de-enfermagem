Introduction to Neurological Scales

The neurological scales constitute one of the fundamental pillars of clinical assessment in nursing, neurology, emergency, and intensive care. They allow objective quantification of the patient's neurological status, transforming qualitative observations into numerical data comparable over time — essential for early detection of clinical deterioration and for precise communication among multidisciplinary team members.

The nervous system is particularly vulnerable to various clinical conditions: traumatic brain injury (TBI), stroke (cerebrovascular accident) ischemic or hemorrhagic, central nervous system infections, metabolic encephalopathies, brain tumors e intracranial hypertension (ICH). In all these scenarios, serial neurological assessment is indispensable.

Different scales were developed for different purposes: some are aimed at rapid screening (FAST, Cincinnati), others for comprehensive assessment of consciousness (Glasgow, FOUR), and still others for quantification of neurological deficit severity (NIHSS). The choice of the correct instrument depends on the clinical context, the phase of care, and available resources.

Professionals who routinely use these scales include nurses (in serial assessment and physical examination), emergency physicians and neurologists (in therapeutic decision), physiotherapists (in functional assessment), and SAMU and prehospital care first responders (in prehospital screening).

Below, we present a comprehensive overview of the main neurological scales, with comparative tables, clinical decision flowchart, and direct access to the project's interactive calculators.

Scales and Calculators

Glasgow Coma Scale

Assessment of level of consciousness

Gold standard for neurological assessment. Evaluates eye opening (1-4), verbal response (1-5), and motor response (1-6). Total score from 3 to 15. Classifies TBI severity.

Open Glasgow

FOUR Scale

Advanced neurological assessment

Full Outline of UnResponsiveness. Assesses 4 domains: eye response, motor response, brainstem reflexes, and respiratory pattern. Ideal for intubated ICU patients. Score from 0 to 16.

Open FOUR

FAST (Stroke Screening)

Rapid stroke screening

Face, Arms, Speech, Time. Prehospital stroke screening. Any abnormality in one of the 3 parameters = immediate activation of stroke protocol. Time is brain.

Open FAST

Cincinnati

Prehospital stroke screening

Cincinnati Prehospital Stroke Scale. Assesses facial asymmetry (smile), unilateral arm drift, and speech abnormality. Positive if 1+ items are abnormal.

Open Cincinnati

NIHSS

National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale

Assesses stroke severity in 11 neurological domains. Score from 0 to 42. Essential for thrombolysis decision (4.5h window). Serial monitoring of evolution.

Open NIHSS

CAM-ICU

Confusion Assessment Method for ICU

Detection of delirium in critically ill patients. Assesses 4 features: acute/fluctuating change, inattention, disorganized thinking, and altered level of consciousness. Validated for mechanical ventilation.

Open CAM-ICU

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between the Glasgow Coma Scale and the FOUR Score?
The Glasgow Coma Scale assesses three parameters (eye opening, verbal response, and motor response) with a score from 3 to 15, being limited in intubated patients who cannot verbalize. The FOUR Score assesses four parameters (eye response, motor response, brainstem reflexes, and respiration pattern) with a score from 0 to 16, being more suitable for intubated patients and allowing brainstem assessment. FOUR is preferable in the ICU when the patient is on mechanical ventilation, as it does not rely on verbal response and includes assessment of brainstem reflexes — which are essential for detecting impending cerebral herniation.
What does FAST mean in stroke assessment?
FAST is an acronym for Face (facial asymmetry — ask the patient to smile), Arms (arm weakness — ask them to raise both arms), Speech (speech difficulty — ask them to repeat a simple phrase), and Time (time to act — note the time of symptom onset). If any of the first three items is abnormal, the patient should be immediately referred to a stroke center, because "time is brain" — each minute without treatment can mean the loss of approximately 1.9 million neurons.
When to use the NIHSS?
The NIHSS (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale) is the gold standard scale for quantifying the severity of acute stroke, with a score from 0 to 42. It should be applied on admission of the patient with suspected stroke, before the decision for thrombolysis (usually indicated when NIHSS is between 4 and 25), and serially every 15 minutes during the infusion of the thrombolytic and in the first 24 hours to monitor progression. NIHSS 0-4 = mild stroke; 5-15 = moderate; 16-20 = moderate to severe; ≥ 21 = severe. It includes assessment of level of consciousness, gaze, visual fields, facial palsy, motor strength, ataxia, sensation, language, dysarthria, and extinction/neglect.
Which neurological scale to use in an intubated patient?
In intubated patients, the FOUR Score is preferable to the Glasgow Coma Scale because: (1) it does not rely on verbal response (which is absent in the intubated patient); (2) it assesses brainstem reflexes — pupillary and corneal — which are essential for detecting cerebral herniation; and (3) it assesses the respiratory pattern, which can indicate the level of neurological dysfunction. It is still possible to apply the Glasgow in intubated patients, recording the verbal response as "T" (tube) or assigning a score of 1, but the assessment loses precision. In practice, many services apply both scales in a complementary manner.
