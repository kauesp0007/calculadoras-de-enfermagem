Introduction to Emergency and Urgency Scales

In the emergency emergency and urgency, time is the most critical resource. Every minute counts — especially in conditions such as Stroke, where neuronal loss can reach 1.9 million neurons per minute without treatment. In this context, rapid screening and assessment scales are indispensable tools for agile and accurate decision-making.

The scales used in the emergency setting differ from those applied in other hospital contexts for a fundamental reason: they need to be rapid (ideally less than 1 minute), simple (applicable by any trained professional, including first responders) and sensitive (capable of detecting serious conditions with high probability).

The concept of "golden hour" in trauma and the 4.5-hour therapeutic window in ischemic stroke illustrate the importance of these tools. FAST (Face, Arms, Speech, Time) and the Cincinnati Scale are examples of instruments developed specifically for the prehospital environment, allowing SAMU responders to identify a stroke while still in the ambulance and direct the patient directly to a specialized center.

In the emergency department, scales such as the NIHSS refine the initial assessment, while the CURB-65 defines the severity of pneumonias and the SOFA identifies organ dysfunction in sepsis. The emergency nurse is a key player in this process, being responsible for the correct application and systematic recording of these scales.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between FAST and Cincinnati?
Both are prehospital stroke screening scales with 3 items and high sensitivity (>80%). FAST was developed in the UK and its acronym stands for Face, Arms, Speech, Time — where the "T" is not an assessment item but a reminder to record the time of symptom onset. The Cincinnati Scale (CPSS) was developed in the USA and assesses the same parameters with slight differences in description: asymmetric smile, unilateral arm drift, and abnormal speech. In practice, they are interchangeable — the key is that any positive result (1 or more altered items) immediately activates the stroke protocol and referral to a specialized center.
When to apply CURB-65 in the emergency department?
CURB-65 should be applied to every patient with suspected community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in the emergency department. The 5 criteria: Confusion (altered mental status), Urea > 50 mg/dL, Respiratory Rate ≥ 30 breaths/min, Blood pressure (systolic < 90 or diastolic ≤ 60 mmHg) and Age ≥ 65 years. Each item = 1 point. Score 0-1: safe outpatient treatment; score 2: consider hospital admission; score ≥ 3: severe pneumonia, indication for ICU admission. Early application reduces decision time and is associated with lower mortality.
What is the nurse's role in applying FAST?
The nurse is often the first professional to assess a patient with suspected stroke, whether in SAMU, risk classification, or the emergency department. Responsibilities include: applying FAST immediately, recording the exact time of symptom onset (or last known well), communicating the result to the medical team, preparing the patient for CT, collecting laboratory tests, checking capillary blood glucose (hypoglycemia can mimic stroke) and monitoring vital signs. After thrombolysis, the nurse applies the NIHSS every 15 minutes during infusion and for the first 2 hours, being essential to detect early neurological deterioration.
