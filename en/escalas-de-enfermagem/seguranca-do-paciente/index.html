Introduction to Patient Safety

The hospital falls represent the most common adverse event related to patient safety in inpatient units, with an incidence ranging from 1.3 to 8.9 falls per 1,000 patient-days. About 30% result in injury, and 5% in serious injuries such as fractures and subdural hematomas. The World Health Organization (WHO), the Joint Commission International and the National Patient Safety Program (PNSP/Ministry of Health) establish fall prevention as one of the six international safety goals.

The first step in prevention is risk identification. The Morse Scale (Morse Fall Scale, 1989) is the gold standard for assessing fall risk in hospitalized adult patients. With 6 items and a score from 0 to 125, it classifies risk as low (0-24), moderate (25-45), and high (> 45). The Downton Scale (1993) is more used in geriatric and long-term care contexts.

The integration of fall risk scales with other assessments — such as Braden (pressure injury), the Barthel (functionality) and the GDS (depression in the elderly) — allows a multidimensional approach to patient safety.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between Morse and Downton?
Morse (6 items, 0-125): assesses fall history (25 pts), secondary diagnosis (15), walking aid (0-30), intravenous/saline access (20), gait (0-20), and mental status (15). > 45 = high risk. It is the hospital gold standard, more sensitive for acute patients. Downton (5 items, 0-11): assesses previous falls (1), medications (1 per class), sensory deficit (1), mental status (1), and gait (1). ≥ 3 = risk. Simpler and aimed at geriatrics/long-term care.
How to implement a fall prevention program?
Implement: (1) assessment with Morse on admission and reassessment every 48h; (2) visual identification (yellow bracelet and bed sign); (3) universal measures — low and locked bed, bed rails, lighting, call bell within reach, non-slip floor; (4) specific measures by risk — Morse > 45: companion, supervision for bathing, physical therapy; (5) medication review (benzodiazepines, antihypertensives, diuretics); (6) reporting every fall and root cause analysis. Monitor fall rate as a quality indicator.
