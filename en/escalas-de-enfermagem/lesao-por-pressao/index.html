Introduction to Pressure Injury Prevention

A pressure injury (PI) is one of the most prevalent adverse events in the hospital environment, with an incidence ranging from 3% to 30% depending on the patient profile and unit. In addition to the human impact — pain, infection, prolonged hospitalization, and in severe cases, septicemia and death — PIs represent a significant financial cost to the healthcare system and are considered a preventable adverse event by WHO and the Joint Commission International.

Prevention begins with systematic risk assessment on patient admission and its periodic reassessment. The Braden Scale, developed by Barbara Braden and Nancy Bergstrom in 1987, is the most validated and widely used instrument worldwide for this purpose. It assesses six subscales — sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, and friction/shear — generating a score from 6 to 23, where the lower the value, the higher the risk.

In the surgical context, ELPO (Risk Assessment Scale for the Development of Injuries Resulting from Surgical Positioning) is the specific instrument for the perioperative period, considering factors such as type of positioning, surgical time, type of anesthesia, and support surface. The surgical patient is particularly vulnerable due to prolonged immobility, anesthesia-induced hypotension, and inability to perceive sensation during the procedure.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between Braden and ELPO?
Braden is universal for clinical and surgical patients, assessing 6 subscales: sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, and friction/shear (score 6-23). ELPO is specific for the perioperative period, with 7 items: type of positioning, surgery time, type of anesthesia, support surface, limb position, comorbidities, and age (score 7-35). ELPO ≥ 19 = high risk. In practice, Braden is applied on admission for all patients, and ELPO is additionally applied to surgical patients preoperatively.
How to interpret the Braden Scale score?
Braden 19-23 = no risk. 15-18 = mild risk (standard prevention: repositioning every 2 hours, skin hydration, viscoelastic mattress). 13-14 = moderate risk (pressure redistribution surface, nutritional assessment, protective barriers). 10-12 = high risk (air mattress, repositioning schedules, prophylactic dressings on bony prominences). ≤ 9 = very high risk (alternating pressure air mattress, daily assessment, intensive nutritional support). Reassessment should occur every 48 hours or upon significant clinical change.
