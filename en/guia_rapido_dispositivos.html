Invasive Devices, Catheters, and Tubes

Reference guide for selection, indications, sizes, and best practices in nursing.

1. Vascular Access Devices (Intravenous and Arterial)

Device Type Gauge (G/Fr) Dwell Time Recommended Use/Flow Rate Advantages Contraindications Major Complications
Central Venous Catheter (CVC)
 4Fr to 12Fr
(Single- to Triple-Lumen)		 Short-term use
(days to weeks)		 Vasoactive drugs, continuous sedation, NPT, PVC monitoring. Access to large-caliber veins. Multiple independent lines. Severe coagulopathy, active infection at the puncture site. Pneumothorax at the insertion site, bloodstream infection (BSI).
Peripheral Venous Catheter (Jelco/Abocath Over-the-Needle)
 14G to 24G 72 to 96 hours
(or daily clinical evaluation)		 Hydration, IV medications, transfusion.
Flow rate: from 20 mL/min (24G) to 240 mL/min (14G).		 Flexible plastic cannula allows for mobility. Quick insertion. Prolonged use of vesicant drugs or NPT. Phlebitis, infiltration, extravasation, and local infection.
Needle Catheter (Scalp)
 19G to 27G Immediate
use (minutes to a few hours)		 Blood collection, rapid single-dose infusion (bolus). Fine metal needle, excellent for veins that are difficult to puncture. Continuous infusions (high risk of endothelial damage). Rapid infiltration, venous transfixation.
PICC (Peripherally Inserted Central Catheter)
 2Fr to 5Fr Long-term placement
(months to >1 year)		 Chemotherapy, prolonged antibiotic therapy (>6 days), NPT, vesicant therapies. Inserted by a nurse. Lower risk of serious complications during insertion. Severe chronic kidney disease (to preserve the venous network for a fistula). Obstruction, peripheral venous thrombosis, mechanical phlebitis.
Arterial
Catheter		 18G to 22G Short duration of stay
(days)		 Invasive blood pressure monitoring (IBP), serial blood gas analysis. Accurate, continuous blood pressure measurement in critically ill patients. Absence of collateral circulation (negative Allen’s test). Distal limb ischemia, severe bleeding, thrombosis.
Implantable Venous Catheter (Port-a-Cath)
 Various sizes Very long indwelling time
(years)		 Oncology (intermittent chemotherapy), chronic infusions. Completely subcutaneous. Aesthetically pleasing and allows for immersion bathing. Patient refusal of surgical procedure, bacteremia. Extravasation due to puncture failure (Huber needle), infection of the “pocket.”
Hemodialysis Catheter (Shiley/Permcath)
 10Fr to 14Fr Shiley (Days)
Permcath (Months)		 Hemodialysis, Hemofiltration, Plasmapheresis (High Flow: >300 mL/min). Allows for emergency renal replacement therapy. Use for routine drug infusion (prohibited!). Hemorrhage, severe systemic infection, central venous stenosis.
Umbilical Catheter (Newborn)
 2.5Fr to 5.0Fr Short-term use
(up to 14 days max)		 Immediate access in the Neonatal ICU: arterial blood gas analysis and venous central venous pressure (CVP) monitoring. Painless for the newborn; eliminates the need for repeated peripheral punctures. Omphalitis, necrotizing enterocolitis. Lower limb ischemia, thrombosis, hemorrhage.

2. Tubes and Drains (Gastrointestinal, Urinary, and Respiratory)

Device Type Gauge (Fr) Dwell Time Recommended Use Advantages Contraindications Major Complications
Nasogastric/Nasoenteral
Catheter		 8Fr to 20Fr SNG (Days)
SNE (Weeks)		 SNG: Decompression, gastric lavage.
SNE: Exclusive enteral nutrition.		 NE with a weight and guide wire carries a lower risk of aspiration (post-pyloric position). Severe facial trauma, skull base fracture. Bronchial aspiration, injury, and necrosis of the nasal mucosa.
Indwelling Bladder Catheter (2/3-way Foley)
 10Fr to 24Fr Days to weeks Monitoring of urine output, urinary retention. 3-way catheter used for continuous irrigation. Balloon ensures secure placement. Closed system ideal for fluid management in the ICU. Pelvic trauma with suspected urethral injury or rupture. Urinary Tract Infection (UTI), urethral trauma due to catheter withdrawal.
Relief Bladder Catheter (Nelaton)
 8Fr to 14Fr Immediate
use (empty and remove)		 Relief of bladder distension, collection of sterile urine samples, postoperative care. Low risk of UTI compared to indwelling catheters. Quick procedure. Acute urethral trauma, undiagnosed urethral bleeding. False urethral tract, bleeding, pain.
Suction
Catheter		 6Fr to 16Fr 10 to 15 sec.
(Single-use)		 Removal of secretions from the VAS or via an endotracheal tube/tracheostomy tube. Prevention of secretion plugs and improvement of patient ventilation. Acute epiglottitis (risk of spasm), severe tracheal fistulas. Hypoxia, arrhythmias, mucosal injury, bronchospasm.
Chest
Drain		 10Fr to 40Fr Until resolution of the condition Drainage of pneumothorax, pleural effusion, empyema, or hemothorax. Restores negative pressure in the pleural space, re-expanding the lung. Severe coagulopathies (assess risk/benefit). Subcutaneous emphysema, infection, drain obstruction, lung injury.

Best Nursing Practices

  • Aseptic Technique: Hand hygiene and rigorous skin antisepsis (alcohol-based chlorhexidine is the gold standard).

  • Push-Pause Technique: Perform a flush (with saline) before and after administering medications (turbulent flow system).

  • System Safety: Keep urinary catheters and drains closed, airtight, and always below the insertion level.

  • Daily Inspection: Assess signs of inflammation every day. If the access is no longer necessary, remove it.

  • Standard Labeling: Every dressing must include: Name of the person who inserted it, date, gauge, and time.

Gauge Selection

Trauma / Emergency: 14G (Orange) or 16G (Gray). Flow rate up to 240 mL/min.
Adults / Major Surgeries: 18G (Green) or 20G (Pink). Excellent flow rate, lower risk.
Pediatrics / Elderly: 22G (Blue) or 24G (Yellow). Preservation of the venous network.

Golden Rule: The HIGHER the Gauge (G), the thinner the diameter. The HIGHER the French (Fr), the thicker the device (1 Fr ≈ 0.33 mm).
